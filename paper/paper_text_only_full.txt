## Chunk 1 of 5 - Abstract and framing

We introduce DHRP, a differentiable extension of Hierarchical Risk Parity, and DHRP-8, a multi-universe benchmark for portfolio allocation across eight ETF asset classes. Classical Hierarchical Risk Parity is attractive because it allocates capital through a tree of asset clusters rather than relying on unstable estimates of expected returns. Its limitation is that the clustering, quasi-diagonalization, and recursive bisection steps are hard, non-differentiable operations. DHRP replaces these discrete steps with a temperature-scaled soft-gating tree, a learned leaf assignment, and an inverse-volatility allocation blend. The result is an HRP-inspired allocation layer that can be trained end to end while preserving the structural bias that makes HRP useful in low-correlation and cross-asset regimes.

The paper should be read as a differentiable relaxation, not as a claim that a learned hard-routing tree exactly reproduces the full classical HRP algorithm. Under restrictive assumptions, a fixed HRP-like allocation can be represented by matching the split budgets, leaf assignments, and inverse-volatility components. The implemented model is broader: it learns an amortized allocation map from price features and covariance matrices to long-only portfolio weights.

DHRP-8 evaluates eight headline methods across developed markets, emerging markets, commodities, sectors, global multi-asset ETFs, factor ETFs, crypto ETFs, and bonds. The benchmark reports out-of-sample Sharpe ratios, Probabilistic Sharpe Ratios, Fama-French factor regressions, transaction-cost sensitivity, and multi-seed neural results. DHRP achieves its strongest results in commodities and crypto, with out-of-sample Sharpe ratios of 1.304 plus or minus 0.095 on commodities and 1.290 plus or minus 0.052 on crypto. The corresponding Probabilistic Sharpe Ratios are 0.999 and 0.991. DHRP beats classical HRP in six of eight universes. The two HRP losses, sectors and factors, are small, both within roughly 0.03 Sharpe.

The results are intentionally nuanced. DHRP does not dominate every universe. Mean-variance allocation is strongest in emerging markets, sectors, and global multi-asset portfolios, where factor timing and equity-heavy exposure appear more important than hierarchical risk budgeting. Commodity Fama-French 3-factor alpha for DHRP is positive but not significant: 6.3 percent annualized with a t-statistic of 1.58. The most defensible headline is therefore robust positive sample Sharpe in selected low-correlation regimes, not statistically significant factor alpha across all conditions.

We also test an LLM-DHRP extension using a global FinBERT summary of asset-attributed headlines. The text signal is constructed from rolling per-asset headline embeddings, normalized and aggregated into a global timestep summary before fusion with price and covariance features. This LLM-DHRP variant does not improve DHRP under multi-seed reporting. It regresses by about 0.57 Sharpe in commodities and about 0.21 Sharpe in developed markets, and is approximately tied in emerging markets. This is an honest negative ablation: it shows that a global text summary is insufficient to improve this allocator under the benchmark protocol, not that all per-asset or retrieval-augmented text architectures are useless.

The contribution is a practical combination of model architecture, benchmark design, statistical discipline, and reproducibility infrastructure. The package includes code, metadata, cached headline features, trained checkpoints, result CSVs, and a single-command reproduction path. The intended message is not that DHRP is universally best, but that differentiable hierarchical allocation is a useful inductive bias and that portfolio-learning papers need multi-universe, multi-seed, cost-aware evaluation.

## Chunk 2 of 5 - Introduction and related work

Portfolio allocation is a difficult target for end-to-end learning. The training signal is noisy, the out-of-sample objective is non-stationary, and small estimation errors in means and covariances can dominate performance. Mean-variance optimization is theoretically elegant, but in finite samples it is highly sensitive to expected-return estimation and covariance instability. Risk-budgeting methods such as equal weight, minimum variance, risk parity, maximum diversification, and Hierarchical Risk Parity are often more robust because they use structure or constraints to reduce estimation burden.

Hierarchical Risk Parity is especially relevant because it uses the covariance structure to organize assets into a hierarchy and then allocates capital recursively across clusters. In practice, this avoids some of the instability of unconstrained mean-variance optimization. However, classical HRP is not differentiable. It depends on agglomerative clustering, a discrete asset ordering, and recursive bisection. These steps make it difficult to train HRP jointly with neural feature extractors, text models, or decision-focused objectives.

Recent machine-learning work has made optimization layers, sorting approximations, and decision trees differentiable. Differentiable optimization layers allow downstream decisions to influence upstream representations. Soft decision trees and neural routing models replace hard splits with temperature-controlled probabilities. Mixture-of-experts models use gating functions to route examples to specialized submodels. DHRP adapts these ideas to portfolio allocation by replacing HRP’s hard tree construction with a learnable soft tree that maps price and covariance features directly into portfolio weights.

The paper’s positioning is important. DHRP is not a generic convex optimizer and it is not a literal differentiable implementation of every step in classical HRP. It is a soft hierarchical allocation layer that preserves the useful inductive bias of tree-structured risk budgeting. This distinction should remain explicit throughout the paper because reviewers may object to any overclaim that the architecture exactly recovers classical HRP.

The benchmark contribution is equally important. Finance-ML papers are vulnerable to single-universe overfitting, single-seed reporting, transaction-cost omissions, and post-hoc factor exposure. DHRP-8 is designed to reduce those problems. It evaluates multiple asset universes, separates deterministic baselines from neural multi-seed results, reports Probabilistic Sharpe Ratios, includes factor regressions, and tests text augmentation under the same protocol.

The LLM result should be framed as a benchmark finding, not as a failure of language models in finance. Finance-domain language models such as FinBERT and broader financial foundation models can extract useful sentiment or event information from text. The negative result here is narrower: a global rolling summary of asset-attributed headlines, fused into the DHRP allocation layer, does not improve multi-seed portfolio performance. This finding aligns with recent benchmark work showing that LLM improvements in financial or time-series tasks are highly protocol-dependent and can disappear under stronger evaluation.

## Chunk 3 of 5 - Method and benchmark

At each rebalance date, DHRP receives a vector of price-derived features and an annualized covariance matrix. The feature vector contains rolling returns, volatility estimates, momentum, volatility ratios, average correlation, and volume aggregates. The covariance matrix is symmetrized and regularized for numerical stability. A neural projection combines the feature representation with covariance-derived information and passes the result into a soft binary tree.

Each internal node of the tree emits a two-way split probability. A temperature parameter controls the sharpness of the split. At high temperature, routing is diffuse and many leaves receive mass. At lower temperature, the tree becomes more decisive. Leaf budgets are mapped to assets through a learned row-stochastic asset-to-leaf assignment. The resulting allocation is blended with an inverse-volatility allocation so that the model retains a risk-budgeting prior even when neural gates are uncertain. The final output is a long-only weight vector that sums to one.

Training uses a decision-focused allocation objective. The loss combines smooth Sharpe-style performance, utility terms, diversification pressure, covariance risk, turnover regularization, and a soft HRP-alignment penalty. The intent is to train the model on portfolio outcomes rather than on imitation of a single classical allocation. Hyperparameters are fixed before the multi-seed runs, and deterministic baselines are evaluated without seed variation.

The HRP connection should be stated carefully. DHRP is HRP-inspired because it allocates through a hierarchy and retains inverse-volatility structure. It can represent HRP-like allocations under restrictive assumptions involving a fixed covariance instance, a fixed tree and asset order, enough leaves for all assets, one-hot asset-to-leaf assignment, split budgets aligned with HRP recursive bisection, and the appropriate inverse-volatility blend. The implemented hard-routing limit does not claim to reproduce arbitrary agglomerative clustering. This narrower statement is mathematically safer and better aligned with the code.

LLM-DHRP adds a text pathway only where headline features are available. For each asset and date, the system computes rolling mean FinBERT headline embeddings. The per-asset embeddings are normalized, aggregated by cross-sectional mean and max pooling, and concatenated into a global text summary for the timestep. That summary is fused with the DHRP representation through a gated residual pathway. This design tests whether a global FinBERT summary improves allocation. It does not test full per-asset text-token attention, retrieval-augmented reasoning, or event-level trading logic.

DHRP-8 uses eight fixed ten-asset ETF universes: developed markets, emerging markets, commodities, sectors, global multi-asset, factor ETFs, crypto ETFs, and bonds. The out-of-sample period is July 2020 through April 2026, with a five-day purge gap between training and testing windows to reduce look-ahead risk. Crypto has a shorter usable history because ETF availability is shorter. Some commodity and factor ETFs have late inceptions, so missingness is measured before imputation and assets below the coverage threshold are excluded from affected windows. Neural methods are run in strict mode to avoid silently replacing failed model outputs with equal-weight portfolios.

The headline comparison includes equal weight, mean-variance, minimum variance, maximum diversification, risk parity, classical HRP, DHRP, and LLM-DHRP where text is available. Additional implemented neural baselines include MLP, Transformer, PPO, and decision-focused learning variants; these are best treated as supplementary or appendix experiments unless all are reported across all universes.

## Chunk 4 of 5 - Results and interpretation

DHRP is strongest in commodities and crypto. In commodities, DHRP reaches a mean out-of-sample Sharpe of 1.304 with standard deviation 0.095 over ten seeds and a Probabilistic Sharpe Ratio of 0.999. The best non-DHRP commodity baseline is risk parity with Sharpe 1.106. Classical HRP reaches 0.971. In crypto, DHRP reaches Sharpe 1.290 with standard deviation 0.052 and Probabilistic Sharpe Ratio 0.991. The best non-DHRP crypto baseline is mean-variance with Sharpe 1.107, followed by equal weight at 1.094 and risk parity at 1.079.

In developed markets, DHRP is also the strongest headline method, with Sharpe 0.446 compared with mean-variance at 0.333 and classical HRP at 0.216. In bonds, all methods are weak because the test period is difficult for fixed-income ETFs, but DHRP loses less than the alternatives and beats classical HRP. DHRP also beats HRP in emerging markets and global multi-asset portfolios, although it does not beat the best mean-variance result in those universes.

The main caveat is that DHRP does not dominate equity-heavy or factor-dominated universes. Mean-variance is best in emerging markets, sectors, and global multi-asset portfolios. In sectors, DHRP’s Sharpe is 0.677, while HRP is 0.698 and mean-variance is 0.861. In factor ETFs, DHRP’s Sharpe is 0.682 while HRP is 0.704. These losses are small relative to HRP but important for interpretation. They show that hierarchical risk budgeting is not universally superior; it is regime-dependent and most useful when cross-asset correlation structure provides a stable allocation signal.

The factor-regression results should be presented conservatively. Commodity Fama-French 3-factor alpha for DHRP is 6.3 percent annualized with a HAC t-statistic of 1.58, so it is not significant at the 5 percent level. Minimum variance and LLM-DHRP clear the 5 percent threshold in the commodity factor table, but they do not improve the DHRP headline multi-seed Sharpe. This distinction matters. DHRP’s headline result is driven by sample Sharpe and positive out-of-sample performance, not by robust evidence of significant factor alpha.

The LLM-DHRP ablation is negative. In developed markets, DHRP has Sharpe 0.446 and LLM-DHRP has 0.239, a decline of 0.207. In emerging markets, DHRP has 0.469 and LLM-DHRP has 0.474, a negligible difference. In commodities, DHRP has 1.304 and LLM-DHRP has 0.738, a decline of 0.566. The most plausible explanation is that ETF-level allocation already captures broad market information through returns, volatility, covariance, and volume, while rolling headline embeddings add noisy or weakly attributable information. The global mean and max pooling also removes asset-specific attribution, which may be necessary for text to help allocation.

Transaction costs are material. For the commodity DHRP strategy, net Sharpe falls from 1.30 at zero basis points to 1.21 at 5 basis points, 1.12 at 10 basis points, 0.93 at 25 basis points, and 0.51 at 50 basis points. The strategy remains attractive through moderate costs, but the claim is cost-sensitive rather than cost-invariant. This should be emphasized to avoid overstatement.

The safest empirical conclusion is that DHRP is a strong HRP-family method in low-correlation and cross-asset regimes, especially commodities and crypto, while mean-variance remains competitive or superior in factor-dominated equity universes. DHRP-8 is therefore more useful as a diagnostic benchmark than as a single leaderboard designed to make one method win everywhere.

## Chunk 5 of 5 - Reproducibility, limitations, conclusion, and submission positioning

The artifact is designed for auditability. The release includes a flat Python package, a reproduction script, cached headline and macro features, trained checkpoints, result CSVs, figure-generation utilities, Croissant metadata, and an anonymization scanner. The reproduction script verifies imports, model forward passes, dataset construction, and backtest execution before running the full experiment. The package also removes private environment files, local caches, Git history, notebook execution outputs, and personal tokens from the submission archive.

The main limitations should be stated plainly. The ETF universes are fixed, so survivorship and selection bias remain. Crypto history is shorter than the other universes. Several commodity and factor ETFs have late inceptions, requiring explicit coverage filtering. The backtests are historical and should not be interpreted as live-trading evidence or investment advice. Transaction costs are modeled through turnover debits, but execution quality, bid-ask spread dynamics, borrow constraints, futures roll structure, and market impact are not fully modeled. The text model uses global FinBERT summaries, not per-asset retrieval, per-headline reasoning, or event-level causal attribution.

There are also statistical limitations. Probabilistic Sharpe Ratio is useful evidence that a method’s Sharpe is positive, but it is not a paired test against a specific baseline. Claims about outperforming HRP, risk parity, or mean-variance should be supported by paired stationary-bootstrap confidence intervals or multiple-comparison-aware tests. The current paper should avoid strong wording such as “significantly outperforms” unless the paired tests support it. The correct wording is that DHRP has higher mean out-of-sample Sharpe than HRP in six of eight universes and is strongest in commodities and crypto under the reported protocol.

For the nearest currently open venue, the best fit is ICAIF 2026, the ACM International Conference on AI in Finance. The paper is directly aligned with the conference’s AI-and-finance scope: portfolio allocation, financial benchmarks, decision-focused learning, LLM-finance ablation, reproducible evaluation, and responsible interpretation of backtested results. The ICAIF version should emphasize the benchmark and applied AI contribution, not only the model. It should stay within the ACM eight-page total limit including references, avoid supplementary material if the call disallows it, and preserve anonymity.

For a later AAAI-style version, the paper should be strengthened around general AI contributions: differentiable hierarchical routing, decision-focused learning under noisy objectives, benchmark design, and negative evidence for cross-modal LLM augmentation. A general AI venue will likely demand stronger algorithmic novelty and more rigorous statistical testing than a finance-specialized venue. The AAAI version should add paired bootstrap tests, clearer ablation tables, a stronger architecture comparison, and possibly a true per-asset text-attention model if the paper wants to make broader claims about LLMs.

The final conclusion should be measured. DHRP makes an HRP-like allocation bias differentiable, but it does not solve portfolio learning universally. It performs especially well in commodities and crypto, where low-correlation or cross-asset structure makes hierarchical risk budgeting valuable. It is competitive but not dominant in other universes, and it loses to mean-variance in several equity-heavy settings. The LLM-DHRP negative ablation is valuable because it prevents overclaiming and shows that global text summaries are insufficient under multi-seed evaluation. Overall, the paper’s strongest contribution is the combination of a differentiable hierarchical allocator, a multi-universe benchmark, corrected statistical framing, and a reproducible artifact that can support future work on AI-driven portfolio allocation.
