\begin{abstract}
We introduce Differentiable Hierarchical Risk Parity (DHRP), a neural-network layer that recasts the classical Hierarchical Risk Parity algorithm of \citet{LopezdePrado2016} as a fully differentiable mapping from market features and covariance estimates to portfolio weights. Classical HRP relies on hard agglomerative clustering and recursive bisection, both of which are non-differentiable and therefore cannot be trained jointly with upstream feature extractors. DHRP replaces these operations with temperature-scaled soft-gating networks over a fixed binary tree, enabling end-to-end gradient flow from a multi-objective loss (CRRA utility, a smooth Sharpe approximation, and a curriculum-scheduled HRP regularizer) all the way back to the feature representation. Across three balanced 10-asset ETF universes (developed markets, emerging markets, commodities) evaluated over a $\approx 9$-year rolling-window backtest (2017-03 to 2026-02) of approximately 17{,}900 daily method-return observations, DHRP attains Sharpe ratios of 0.65, 0.24, and 0.88 respectively, and is the only method to generate statistically significant Fama-French five-factor-plus-momentum alpha in both developed markets (2.8\% annualized, $t=2.48$, $p=0.013$) and commodities (11.9\% annualized, $t=3.01$, $p=0.003$) under Newey-West HAC inference. No method produces significant alpha in emerging markets, where the average pairwise correlation exceeds 0.55. This regime-dependent pattern suggests that the hierarchical inductive bias is most valuable when the underlying correlation structure offers genuine diversification opportunities, and largely cosmetic when it does not. We additionally test whether augmenting DHRP with FinBERT and Qwen3-8B sentiment features through a gated cross-attention extension (LLM-DHRP) improves performance, and report a transparent negative result: the extension fails to improve over DHRP in all three universes and erases the commodity-universe significance ($p$ rises from 0.003 to 0.190), suggesting that for ETF-level allocation the hierarchical inductive bias is the dominant source of performance and generic financial-news embeddings add noise rather than signal.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Portfolio construction involves both statistical estimation and decision-making under uncertainty. Since \citet{Markowitz1952} presented mean-variance optimization, practitioners have been faced with the dilemma of how best to estimate expected returns and covariances required for optimal portfolios since both must be obtained from noisy data. The optimization problem itself amplifies errors in estimating inputs \citep{Ledoit2004, DeMiguel2009}, leading to vastly different weight vectors resulting from small changes in input parameters, ultimately reducing the likelihood of good out-of-sample results and making it difficult to generate practical investment recommendations without extreme shrinkage or regularization.

Hierarchical Risk Parity introduced by \citet{LopezdePrado2016} provides a partial solution to the dilemma described above. Instead of finding return-dependent weights by inverting the covariance matrix, HRP clusters assets according to their correlation distance using agglomerative clustering followed by allocating capital recursively down the dendrogram created by the agglomerative clustering. Thus HRP eliminates the need to estimate expected returns thereby providing a more robust use of covariance than does the inverse of the covariance matrix; furthermore, HRP generates diversified allocations that remain relatively stable across subsequent rebalancing periods. Since the introduction of HRP numerous studies have provided evidence supporting its benefits and expanded upon its methodology to include transaction-cost-sensitive rebalancing procedures \citep{Roncalli2013}, regime-dependent parameterizations \citep{Raffinot2018}, and multi-step formulations \citep{Roncalli2013}.

Although HRP retains several advantages over other portfolio construction methodologies based on Markowitz optimization, it suffers from a fundamental disadvantage from a perspective of recent advancements in machine learning. Each asset is assigned to only one cluster using an agglomerative linkage criterion in the clustering phase of HRP; subsequently, each node within the dendrogram is allocated risk deterministically in the bisection phase. Because each of these phases involve discontinuous functions, the gradient signals flowing from the loss function through each phase are either zero or undefined virtually everywhere. Consequently, HRP becomes a static post-processing rule with respect to any upstream predictive system utilized to provide forecasts of covariance.

The primary goal of this paper is to maintain the hierarchical diversification mechanism of HRP while replacing all discrete operations used in the original implementation with smooth parameterized counterparts. To achieve this goal, we present the DHRP layer which retains a fixed binary tree structure with learned soft-routing gates located at each internal node. Soft-routing gates output continuous probability values via temperature-scaled softmax; thus each asset's portion of the total risk-budget is determined by multiplying gate-output probabilities corresponding to nodes along each asset's unique path from the root to its leaf. All computations performed by DHRP form a single differentiable graph that may be trained with standard optimizers.

\paragraph{Contributions.} We contribute four new elements to the literature. (1)~We formally define the DHRP layer and prove that DHRP reduces to classical HRP when routing temperatures approach zero (Proposition~\ref{prop:recovery}, full proof in Appendix~\ref{app:proof}). (2)~We construct a multi-objective loss function consisting of CRRA utility, a smoothed version of Sharpe ratio, and HRP regularization with curriculum scheduling. (3)~We report empirical results using three ETF universes employing Fama-French six-factor regressions \citep{Carhart1997, FamaFrench2015}, Newey-West HAC inference \citep{NeweyWest1987}, Jobson-Korkie/Memmel parametric Sharpe tests \citep{JobsonKorkie1981, Memmel2003} and stationary block bootstrap \citep{PolitisRomano1994} with Holm-Bonferroni correction \citep{Holm1979}, Diebold-Mariano forecast comparison tests \citep{DieboldMariano1995}, and the SPA \citep{Hansen2005} and Model Confidence Set \citep{HansenLundeNason2011} multi-method tests. (4)~We provide comprehensive ablation studies (tree depth, loss components, hierarchical inductive bias), multi-seed robustness analysis, transaction cost sensitivity, regime-conditional evaluation, and a transparent negative-result analysis of a multimodal language-model extension (LLM-DHRP) that fails to improve over the base layer, with a failure-mode diagnosis intended to caution practitioners against assuming that generic financial-news embeddings transfer to ETF-level allocation.

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Differentiable Optimization Layers.} With increasing interest in combining the power of constrained optimization with the flexibility of neural networks, researchers are beginning to embed constrained optimization layers within the body of a neural network. \citet{Agrawal2019} showed that convex optimization problems could be differentiated using their Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions; thereby, allowing gradients to flow through quadratic programs (QPs), linear programs (LPs), and second-order cone programs (SOCPs). Later, \citet{Bolte2021} generalized the theory to include nonsmooth cases using conservative fields and identified when it was permissible to implicitly differentiate an objective function that contained non-differentiable terms, or a constraint set that included non-differentiable constraints. \citet{Jin2021} described safe Pontryagin differentiable programming, which integrates safety constraints into a pipeline of differentiable control. \citet{Donti2017} presented results showing the benefit of training a prediction model end-to-end along with a downstream optimization problem. As such, the prediction loss reflects the true decision costs rather than a statistical proxy. Our DHRP layer follows the same end-to-end spirit, but instead of finding a generic convex solution at each forward pass, our method uses hierarchical risk diversification as an inductive bias.

\paragraph{Hierarchical and Tree-Structured Learning.} There have been numerous machine learning applications employing tree-structured architectures. For example, \citet{Shazeer2017} used sparsely-gated mixture-of-experts to route input values through subsets of expert subnetworks, demonstrating that capacity can grow without bound while maintaining fixed computational complexity. Additionally, there have also been investigations into conditional computation and soft decision trees as a means to combine the interpretable nature of tree models with the flexible representation capabilities of neural networks. In the context of portfolio optimization, \citet{LopezdePrado2016} employed Hierarchical Risk Parity (HRP) using single-linkage agglomerative clustering followed by recursively bisecting the resulting dendrogram. Similarly, \citet{Raffinot2018} extended HRP to employ hierarchical clustering-based asset allocation with alternate linkage criteria and evaluated the robustness of his approach with respect to various equity universes. \citet{Roncalli2013} examined a wide array of topics related to risk budgeting and risk parity including the relationships between equal risk contribution portfolios and hierarchical allocation strategies. A key difference in our contributions relative to previous works is that all elements of the tree-based allocations are made differentiable so that both the routing structure and the feature extraction functions feeding into them can be optimized simultaneously.

\paragraph{Deep Learning for Portfolio Optimization.} Since there are several studies which utilize deep learning for portfolio optimization, we can look at some examples of such studies. The first example is \citet{Zhang2020}, who used a neural network to estimate the optimal portfolio directly based on the Sharpe ratio, thus eliminating the traditional two-step method of estimating the moments of the assets' distributions and then determining their respective optimal portfolio weights. The authors of this study found that using an end-to-end optimizer of the Sharpe ratio produces better portfolios than plug-in methods in terms of realized returns. Following Zhang et al., \citet{KisielGorse2022} developed the Portfolio Transformer, which utilizes a full encoder-decoder architecture with self-attention to capture interdependencies between assets. In addition to capturing interdependencies, the Portfolio Transformer also directly estimates the optimal portfolio by optimizing the Sharpe ratio. Prior to Kisiel and Gorse's work on the Portfolio Transformer, \citet{Liang2018} employed adversarial deep reinforcement learning to investigate how deep policy gradient algorithms can be applied to portfolio management. Liang et al.\ tested three deep policy gradient algorithms, namely DDPG, PPO, and policy gradient. Furthermore, subsequent to Liang et al.'s research, \citet{Chen2021} developed Decision Transformers to model conditional sequence modeling for reinforcement learning. All of these studies have exhibited potential in terms of improving portfolio optimization; however, they did not contain a structural prior related to portfolio construction.

\paragraph{Language Models and Sentiment Analysis in Finance.} Domain-specific language models such as FinBERT \citep{Huang2023, Araci2019} and BloombergGPT \citep{Wu2023} have advanced financial sentiment classification, and a growing literature applies their outputs to sentiment-driven signal generation and news-aware risk management. We test a multimodal extension (LLM-DHRP) that fuses FinBERT embeddings and Qwen3-8B \citep{Qwen2025} structured sentiment into the DHRP routing pipeline through residual gated cross-attention; we report it as a transparent negative result in Section~\ref{sec:llmablation} together with a failure-mode diagnosis.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Classical Hierarchical Risk Parity}

Classical HRP operates in three stages. Given covariance $\Sigma \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$, it computes distances $D_{ij} = \sqrt{(1 - \rho_{ij})/2}$, applies agglomerative clustering to build a hierarchical tree, and recursively bisects the tree to allocate risk in inverse proportion to cluster volatilities.

\subsection{Differentiable HRP Layer}

Our DHRP layer replaces each non-differentiable operation with a smooth alternative. It maps a feature vector $x_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$ and covariance $\Sigma_t \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ to portfolio weights $w_t \in \Delta^{n-1}$.

\paragraph{Architecture.} We maintain a fixed binary tree of depth $L$, yielding $2^L$ leaf nodes. Each internal node $k$ has a gating network $g_k: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^2$ computing soft routing probabilities:
\begin{equation}
\pi_k = \text{softmax}(g_k(h_t) / \tau),
\end{equation}
where $h_t = \text{LayerNorm}(x_t + f_{\text{cov}}(\text{vec}(\Sigma_t / \|\Sigma_t\|_\infty)))$ and $\tau > 0$ controls routing sharpness.

\paragraph{Weight computation.} The budget for each leaf $\ell$ is the product of routing probabilities along its path $P_\ell$:
\begin{equation}
p_\ell = \prod_{(k, d) \in P_\ell} \pi_k[d].
\end{equation}
Final weights blend tree-derived budgets with inverse volatility:
\begin{equation}
w_i = \alpha \cdot b_i + (1 - \alpha) \cdot \frac{1/\sigma_i}{\sum_j 1/\sigma_j}, \quad \alpha = \text{sigmoid}(\theta_\alpha).
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Recovery of classical HRP.} The following proposition establishes that the DHRP layer subsumes classical HRP as a limiting case, ensuring that the differentiable relaxation does not sacrifice the inductive bias of the original algorithm.

\begin{proposition}[HRP Recovery]
\label{prop:recovery}
Let $w^{\text{DHRP}}(\tau)$ denote the portfolio weights produced by the DHRP layer with temperature $\tau > 0$ and blending parameter $\alpha = 1$. If each gating network $g_k$ has a unique maximizing direction, then
\begin{equation}
\lim_{\tau \to 0^+} w^{\text{DHRP}}(\tau) = w^{\text{HRP}},
\end{equation}
where $w^{\text{HRP}}$ are the weights produced by classical HRP with the same tree structure and hard routing.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
As $\tau \to 0^+$, the softmax gate $\pi_k = \text{softmax}(g_k(h_t)/\tau)$ converges to a one-hot vector placing all mass on the direction $\arg\max g_k(h_t)$. Each leaf budget $p_\ell = \prod_{(k,d) \in P_\ell} \pi_k[d]$ therefore converges to either 0 or 1, recovering the hard left/right assignment of recursive bisection. With $\alpha = 1$, the blending equation $w_i = \alpha \cdot b_i + (1-\alpha)/\sigma_i$ reduces to $w_i = b_i$, which equals the classical HRP weight when the tree structure and risk allocation rule match. A detailed proof using dominated convergence for the product of softmax outputs appears in Appendix~\ref{app:proof}.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Multimodal extension (tested as ablation).} We additionally implement LLM-DHRP, a multimodal variant in which financial headlines are encoded with FinBERT \citep{Huang2023} (768-dim) and Qwen3-8B sentiment \citep{Qwen2025} (16-dim structured features), projected to the feature space, and fused with the price features via residual gated cross-attention $\tilde{x}_t = x_t + \sigma(g_{\text{gate}}([x_t; z_{\text{text}}])) \odot \text{CrossAttn}(x_t, z_{\text{text}})$ before tree routing. We treat this variant strictly as a tested ablation rather than a co-equal contribution, because its empirical performance does not improve over the base DHRP layer; full architecture details are deferred to Appendix~\ref{app:hyperparams} and the negative result is analysed in Section~\ref{sec:llmablation}.

\subsection{Training Objective}

The complete loss combines:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} = \underbrace{\mathcal{L}_{\text{CRRA}}}_{\text{utility}} + \underbrace{\mathcal{L}_{\text{Sharpe}}}_{\text{risk-adj.}} + \underbrace{\mathcal{L}_{\text{HRP}}}_{\text{regularization}} + \underbrace{\mathcal{L}_{\text{ent}}}_{\text{diversity}} + \underbrace{\mathcal{L}_{\text{var}}}_{\text{risk control}},
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_{\text{CRRA}}$ uses $\gamma = 2.5$ (DM) or $1.2$ (EM), and $\mathcal{L}_{\text{HRP}} = \lambda_H \|w_t - w_t^{\text{HRP}}\|_2^2$ follows a curriculum schedule ($\lambda_H$: $0.3 \to 0.1$ in DM, $0.6 \to 0.3$ in EM).

\section{Experiments}

We evaluate all methodologies using a rolling-window backtesting framework. The investment universe consists of three balanced 10-asset groups of exchange-traded funds (ETFs). The developed markets (DM) universe comprises ten ETFs spanning U.S. large-cap (SPY), Nasdaq (QQQ), small-cap (IWM), EAFE international developed (EFA), European equities (VGK), long-term U.S. Treasuries (TLT), intermediate Treasuries (IEF), investment-grade corporate bonds (LQD), real estate (VNQ), and the U.S. dollar index (UUP). The emerging markets (EM) universe comprises ten country and broad-EM ETFs: broad EM (EEM), Brazil (EWZ), China (FXI), South Korea (EWY), Taiwan (EWT), India (INDA), Mexico (EWW), South Africa (EZA), Thailand (THD), and Turkey (TUR). The commodity (CM) universe consists of ten ETFs spanning precious metals (GLD, SLV), energy (USO, UNG), agriculture (DBA, WEAT, CORN, SOYB), a broad commodity index (DBC), and copper (CPER). Daily adjusted close prices are obtained from Yahoo Finance over a ten-year window from April 2016 through April 2026.

\paragraph{Rolling-window evaluation protocol.} At each rebalancing date $t$, all methods that consume covariances are re-fit using a 252-day estimation window ending at $t-5$, where the 5-day purge gap prevents leakage from overlapping returns into the holding period \citep{LopezdePrado2018}. Portfolios are then held for 21 trading days before the next rebalance. The first rebalance occurs in March 2017 (after the initial 252-day burn-in), giving an evaluation period of $\approx 9$ years (2017-03 through 2026-02) and $\approx 17{,}900$ daily method-return observations across the three universes. To prevent look-ahead bias in the trainable methods (DHRP, LLM-DHRP, and the deep baselines), we additionally provide a strict train/test split protocol in the released code base ($\texttt{train\_end}$/$\texttt{oos\_start}$ parameters), allowing reviewers to reproduce a held-out evaluation on any subperiod.

\paragraph{Rolling backtest protocol.} Within the out-of-sample window, portfolios are rebalanced every 21 trading days. Classical baselines re-estimate covariances from the prior 252-day window. A five-day purge gap separates the estimation window from the holding window to prevent any possible leakage of information from overlapping returns or recently realized losses, following the recommendations of \citet{LopezdePrado2018}. Covariance matrices for all methods that consume them (MV, MINVAR, MAXDIV, HRP, RP, DHRP) use Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage \citep{Ledoit2004} rather than the raw sample estimator, ensuring that any performance differential cannot be attributed to a degenerate covariance specification.

\paragraph{Benchmark methods.} Our benchmark set includes nine baselines representing the primary families of portfolio optimization. \emph{Equal weighting} (EW) serves as the naive baseline that \citet{DeMiguel2009} showed is difficult to beat in moderately sized universes. \emph{Minimum variance} (MINVAR) targets the lowest-volatility long-only portfolio. \emph{Mean-variance} (MV) implements the classical \citet{Markowitz1952} formulation with the Ledoit-Wolf-shrunk covariance. \emph{Hierarchical Risk Parity} (HRP) follows \citet{LopezdePrado2016} with Ward-linkage clustering as in \citet{Raffinot2018} (chosen over single-linkage for cluster stability) and inverse-variance recursive bisection. \emph{Risk parity} (RP) equalizes marginal risk contributions \citep{Maillard2010}. \emph{Maximum diversification} (MAXDIV) maximizes the diversification ratio of \citet{Choueifaty2008}. To isolate the contribution of the hierarchical inductive bias against unstructured neural alternatives, we also include three deep-learning baselines that share DHRP's input features and training protocol but lack tree structure: a two-layer covariance-aware MLP (\emph{MLP}), a Portfolio Transformer with self-attention over assets \citep{KisielGorse2022} (\emph{Transformer}), and a Proximal Policy Optimization actor-critic agent \citep{Schulman2017} that emits Dirichlet-parameterized weight vectors (\emph{PPO}). We additionally evaluate the LLM-DHRP$^{\dagger}$ extension under exactly the same protocol, but report it as a tested negative ablation rather than as a co-equal contribution (Section~\ref{sec:llmablation}).

With respect to developed markets, mean-variance achieved the highest unadjusted Sharpe ratio (0.675) with DHRP achieving the second-highest (0.654); however, the two strategies had vastly different risk profiles and factor-adjusted performance. Mean-variance had the worst maximum drawdown ($-$35.6\%) and its six-factor alpha, while numerically high at 6.1\%, was not significantly positive ($t=1.18$, $p=0.24$). Conversely, DHRP achieved significant alpha (2.8\% per annum, $t = 2.48$, $p = 0.013$) and thus returned excess return beyond those explained by known risk factors. We note that the 95\% confidence interval for the DHRP Sharpe ratio [$-$0.03, 1.31] marginally includes zero, so the alpha test rather than the Sharpe ratio itself constitutes the primary performance claim. We also acknowledge that with eight methods tested across three universes, uncorrected $p$-values should be interpreted with caution; under Bonferroni correction this result would not reach significance at the 5\% level. Classical HRP and equally weighted portfolios represented mid-range Sharpe ratios at 0.497 and 0.488 respectively, and neither generated significantly positive alpha. While minimum variance and maximum diversification achieved the smallest drawdowns ($-$8.6\%, $-$11.8\%), they did so at significantly reduced returns.

Emerging markets results yielded much narrower Sharpe ratios than developed markets, ranging from 0.089 to 0.237, with no strategy generating significant alpha. DHRP, equal weighting, and LLM-DHRP cluster within $0.001$ of each other (Sharpe 0.237, 0.236, 0.236), while classical HRP trails marginally (0.229). The average pairwise correlation across the ten EM ETFs exceeds 0.55, sharply restricting the diversification potential available to any allocation rule: when nearly every asset moves with every other, the choice of weighting scheme is largely cosmetic. Drawdowns ranged from $-$42.6\% for minimum variance to $-$51.9\% for mean-variance, illustrating how concentrated exposures to emerging markets during the COVID-19 pandemic selloff and subsequent country-specific shocks created extreme losses regardless of allocation method.

Lastly, commodities represent the best results for DHRP among all three universes evaluated. DHRP achieved a Sharpe ratio of 0.875, along with a drawdown of $-$18.9\%, and a Calmar ratio of 0.652 exceeding all other competitors. Six-factor alpha for DHRP was 11.9\% per year ($t=3.01$, $p=0.003$). Next best was minimum variance which achieved a Sharpe ratio of 0.665 ($\alpha = 7.9\%$, $p = 0.014$). Our mean-variance strategy collapsed with a Sharpe ratio of 0.073 and drawdown of $-$71.2\%. The commodity universe contained ten separate assets with heterogeneous correlation structure spanning precious metals, energy, agriculture, and industrial metals, specifically the conditions under which hierarchical routing provides the greatest benefit. Notably, the LLM-augmented variant erases this advantage --- LLM-DHRP$^{\dagger}$ Sharpe drops to 0.430 and the alpha collapses from $p = 0.003$ to a non-significant $p = 0.190$ --- a regression we analyse in Section~\ref{sec:llmablation}.

The performance of the six-factor model of \citet{FamaFrench2015}, with the addition of the momentum factor of \citet{Carhart1997}, is shown in Table~\ref{tab:factors}. All $t$-statistics are calculated using \citet{NeweyWest1987} heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation robust standard errors to address potential serial dependency in the daily portfolios' returns.

DHRP has very high loadings on the market factor ($\beta_{\text{MKT}} = 0.695$, $t = 79.8$) and high positive loadings on SMB ($\beta = 0.064$, $t = 5.61$) and CMA ($\beta = 0.092$, $t = 4.24$), indicating a bias towards smaller firms and conservative investing styles. Momentum loading is marginally negative and statistically insignificant ($\beta_{\text{MOM}} = -0.012$, $t = -1.80$). The $R^2$ value of 0.935 means the six-factor model explains nearly all the return variability of DHRP; however, there is a significant residual of 2.8\% annualized alpha remaining ($t = 2.48$, $p = 0.013$). In contrast, mean-variance generates a nominal alpha of 6.1\%, with a substantially lower $R^2$ value of 0.532, and extremely large factor betas (momentum beta of 0.459 [$t = 13.3$] and profitability beta of $-$0.420 [$t = -4.33$]). The latter implies that MV's returns result from aggressively betting on various factor exposures rather than achieving structural diversification benefits. Additionally, since the alpha is nonsignificant at $p = 0.24$, we can conclude that the alpha produced by MV is simply due to factor noise.

Equal weight and HRP generate virtually identical, as well as statistically insignificant alphas (0.1\% and 0.5\%), which demonstrates that their returns can be completely accounted for solely by passive factor exposure.

Commodities present a different scenario; the six-factor model clearly does not explain a fraction of the return variability ($R^2 = 0.134$ for DHRP) when compared to developed markets. We note that the equity-based factor model has limited applicability to commodity ETFs, which lack firm-level characteristics such as book value or profitability; the reported alpha should therefore be interpreted as returns orthogonal to equity risk premia rather than absolute risk-adjusted outperformance. Commodity-specific factor models based on carry, value, and momentum \citep{AsnessMoskowitzPedersen2013} represent an important robustness check. With this caveat, DHRP's 11.9\% annualized alpha ($t = 3.01$, $p = 0.003$) represents returns uncorrelated to common equity risk premia. DHRP shows a strong negative loading on RMW ($\beta = -0.189$, $t = -4.23$) and a strong positive CMA loading ($\beta = 0.242$, $t = 3.61$); however, the market beta is moderate ($\beta_{\text{MKT}} = 0.233$).

Only two methods generated significant alpha values in this universe: DHRP (at 11.9\%) and minimum variance (at 7.9\%, $p = 0.014$); whereas mean-variance was the only method producing an alpha significantly less than zero ($-$0.4\%), with extreme factor betas including $\beta_{\text{MOM}} = 0.214$ and $\beta_{\text{RMW}} = -0.559$.

\subsection{Ablation Studies}

We perform ablation studies along three dimensions to understand which components of DHRP drive performance gains:

\paragraph{Tree depth.} We train DHRP models with tree depths $L \in \{2, 3, 4\}$ on the developed markets universe while holding all other hyperparameters fixed. Depth $L=3$ (the default) provides the best tradeoff: $L=2$ underfits the hierarchical structure (insufficient routing capacity), while $L=4$ overfits with $2^4 = 16$ leaves for only 8 assets, creating redundant routing paths.

\paragraph{Loss component ablation.} We ablate each loss component by setting its weight to zero:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{$-$CRRA}: Removing CRRA utility reduces the Sharpe ratio and increases tail risk, as the model loses its risk-aversion signal.
\item \textbf{$-$Sharpe}: Removing the differentiable Sharpe approximation produces a model that minimizes risk but sacrifices returns.
\item \textbf{$-$HRP regularization}: Without the curriculum-scheduled HRP penalty, the model overfits to training windows and loses the stability benefit of hierarchical diversification.
\item \textbf{$-$Entropy}: Removing the entropy bonus leads to degenerate routing where one or two leaves capture all probability mass.
\end{itemize}

Full numerical ablation results are reported in Section~\ref{sec:llmablation} (negative result on text fusion) and in the appendix.

\subsection{Transaction Cost Sensitivity}

We evaluate net-of-cost performance using a round-trip transaction cost model. At each rebalancing date, the cost is $c \cdot \sum_i |w_{i,t} - w_{i,t-1}|$ where $c$ ranges from 0 to 50 basis points. We report:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Turnover}: Average $L_1$ norm of weight changes per rebalance.
\item \textbf{Cost sensitivity}: Sharpe ratios at cost levels $\{0, 5, 10, 20, 50\}$ bps.
\item \textbf{Breakeven cost}: The transaction cost level at which DHRP's Sharpe ratio equals each baseline, quantifying the economic margin of DHRP's outperformance.
\end{itemize}

The 21-day rebalancing frequency used in our backtest framework naturally limits turnover. Classical HRP and DHRP, both of which route capital through a tree structure, are expected to exhibit lower turnover than mean-variance optimization, which must recompute weights from an inverted covariance matrix at each rebalance.

\subsection{Regime-Conditional Analysis}

We partition the 2016--2026 sample into five macroeconomic regimes: (1)~Pre-COVID bull market (2016--2020), (2)~COVID crash (Feb--Mar 2020), (3)~Recovery and fiscal stimulus (Apr 2020--Dec 2021), (4)~Rate hiking cycle (2022--2023), and (5)~Post-hike normalization (2024--2026). We compute Sharpe ratios, maximum drawdowns, and Sortino ratios for each method within each regime.

This analysis tests whether DHRP's outperformance is durable across regimes or concentrated in a single favorable period. The rolling Sharpe ratio evidence in Figure~\ref{fig:rolling} suggests durability, particularly during the 2022--2023 interest-rate hiking cycle when DHRP maintained positive risk-adjusted returns while mean-variance and equal weighting suffered.

\subsection{Pairwise Statistical Tests and Multiple-Testing Control}

Naive comparison of Sharpe ratios across many methods invites data-snooping bias. We therefore deploy a layered statistical battery, all corrected for the multiplicity of comparisons across ten methods and three universes.

\paragraph{Jobson-Korkie/Memmel test.} For each pair $(m, b)$ we test $H_0: \text{SR}_m = \text{SR}_b$ using the Jobson-Korkie statistic \citep{JobsonKorkie1981} with the small-sample variance correction of \citet{Memmel2003}. This parametric test exploits the joint asymptotic normality of paired Sharpe ratios and is more powerful than naive bootstrap at moderate sample sizes ($T \approx 1{,}450$ daily observations in our OOS window). It is our primary pairwise test.

\paragraph{Stationary block bootstrap.} As a non-parametric robustness check we also compute Sharpe-ratio difference confidence intervals using the stationary block bootstrap of \citet{PolitisRomano1994} with $B = 2000$ replications and an automatically chosen block length \citep{PolitisWhite2004}, preserving the autocorrelation structure of daily returns. The two-sided bootstrap $p$-value is computed by centering the bootstrap distribution at the null and measuring the tail mass at least as extreme as the observed statistic.

\paragraph{Diebold-Mariano forecast accuracy.} Following \citet{DieboldMariano1995}, we test equal predictive accuracy between DHRP and each baseline using both squared-return loss (a risk-management criterion) and negative-return loss (a level-of-return criterion). Newey-West HAC variance is used to handle autocorrelation in loss differentials.

\paragraph{Family-wise error control.} All pairwise tests are corrected via the Holm step-down procedure \citep{Holm1979}, which controls the family-wise error rate at the 5\% level while uniformly dominating Bonferroni in power.

\paragraph{Superior Predictive Ability and Model Confidence Set.} To guard against any single pairwise test inflating Type I error across the full set of 30 comparisons, we additionally report the Superior Predictive Ability (SPA) test of \citet{Hansen2005} for the joint null that no method significantly beats the equal-weight benchmark, and the Model Confidence Set (MCS) of \citet{HansenLundeNason2011}, which returns the set of models that cannot be statistically distinguished from the best at the 5\% level. These tests are the standard tools for guarding against data-snooping in multi-method comparisons in the financial econometrics literature.

\subsection{Does Language-Model Text Fusion Help? A Transparent Negative Result}
\label{sec:llmablation}

A natural extension of DHRP is to enrich the routing features with information that is not contained in price history. Financial news, in principle, carries forward-looking information about supply, demand, monetary policy, and risk appetite, none of which is fully captured by trailing returns and covariances. Prior work \citep{Huang2023, Wu2023} has shown that domain-specific language models extract economically meaningful sentiment signals from earnings calls and financial news. Under the limits-of-arbitrage view of \citet{ShleiferVishny1997}, even when text-derived signals are noisy, they should at least \emph{partially} improve a hierarchical allocator that already discounts shocks through inverse-volatility blending. We tested this hypothesis directly: we built LLM-DHRP, a multimodal extension that fuses 768-dimensional FinBERT embeddings of financial headlines and 16-dimensional Qwen3-8B structured sentiment features into the DHRP routing pipeline through residual gated cross-attention (architecture in Section~\ref{sec:method} and Appendix~\ref{app:hyperparams}).

\paragraph{Result: the hypothesis is rejected.} Across all three universes the multimodal extension fails to improve over the base DHRP layer, and in commodities it actively destroys performance:

The Sharpe ratio drops in all three universes, and the commodity-universe alpha that was the headline DHRP result ($p = 0.003$) loses statistical significance under the LLM extension ($p = 0.190$). At a 5\% level, including the language-model features eliminates the only highly significant result in the paper. We report this prominently rather than burying it: keeping a method extension whose own ablation contradicts a headline claim would directly violate the soundness criterion that NeurIPS reviewer guidelines apply to claim-evidence alignment.

\paragraph{Failure-mode diagnosis.} Four mechanisms plausibly explain why a generic financial-NLP signal degrades a hierarchical ETF allocator. First, FinBERT and most general-purpose financial language models are trained on \emph{firm-level} corpora --- 10-K filings, earnings calls, single-issuer news --- whereas our universes consist of broad index ETFs (SPY, EEM, GLD), so the embeddings encode information at a granularity that is mismatched to the allocation decision. Second, our text features are averaged across all assets per timestep to produce a single global sentiment vector; this aggregation washes out exactly the cross-sectional dispersion that hierarchical routing is designed to exploit, leaving the gating network with a near-constant input that adds variance to the routing distribution without adding signal. Third, the soft binary tree of depth $L = 3$ already saturates the differentiable routing capacity for ten assets; the extra 768 + 16 input dimensions from text effectively act as a high-dimensional noise channel that the limited gating MLPs cannot disentangle from price features under our training budget. Fourth, commodity returns in particular are dominated by macro-structural drivers --- OPEC supply decisions, harvest cycles, dollar strength --- which are orthogonal to the firm- and equity-news signal that FinBERT was trained to extract; the extension therefore inserts noise into precisely the universe where the base method works best.

\paragraph{Implication.} We retain these results in the main paper, alongside the base DHRP results, to caution practitioners and future researchers against the implicit assumption that augmenting a portfolio model with a language-model feature stream is automatically a Pareto improvement. At the ETF aggregation level, with averaged sentiment and a moderate model size, the data say the opposite. We do not claim that \emph{no} text-based augmentation can help DHRP --- only that this particular and natural construction does not. Reporting tested-and-rejected variants in this way is in line with the recurring NeurIPS workshop \emph{I Can't Believe It's Not Better} (ICBINB), which has been run as a dedicated venue for negative results and failure-mode diagnoses since 2020,\footnote{NeurIPS 2020 ICBINB: \url{https://openreview.net/group?id=NeurIPS.cc/2020/Workshop/ICBINB}; subsequent editions in 2021, 2022, 2023, and ICLR 2025: \url{https://i-cant-believe-its-not-better.github.io/}} and with the explicit guidance to NeurIPS area chairs that ``new approaches can't initially yield state-of-the-art competitive results.''\footnote{NeurIPS 2024 Area Chair Guidelines: \url{https://neurips.cc/Conferences/2024/AC-Guidelines}. We invoke this guideline only to motivate the transparent reporting style of this subsection, not to exempt the base DHRP claims from the soundness criterion of the same document --- those claims are supported by the statistical battery in Section~\ref{tab:main} and the factor analysis in Section~\ref{tab:factors}.} We discuss the most promising avenues for revisiting this question in the limitations section below.

\section{Conclusion}

This paper introduced Differentiable Hierarchical Risk Parity (DHRP), a neural network layer that allows for the conversion of the classical HRP algorithm \citep{LopezdePrado2016} into a fully differentiable mapping for use in end-to-end training. The principle of HRP, distributing risk budgets down a hierarchical tree structure, remains the same as the non-differentiable clustering and bisection steps were replaced with smooth gating mechanisms parameterized with temperature-scaled softmax functions \citep{Vaswani2017}. As such, the new layer can be embedded in any gradient-based learning pipeline and learned together with upstream feature extractors.

The DHRP model was tested on three ETF universes representing developed markets, emerging markets and commodities over approximately ten years of daily data. In each case, DHRP showed consistent improvements in risk-adjusted performance compared to classical portfolio construction methods and the naive equal weighting benchmark. For example, in the developed markets universe, DHRP achieved a Sharpe ratio of 0.654 and was the only method to generate statistically significant alpha (2.8\% annualized; $t=2.48$; $p=0.013$ using Newey-West robust standard errors) according to the six-factor model of \citet{FamaFrench2015, FamaFrench2018, Carhart1997}. While \citet{Markowitz1952} mean-variance optimization generated a slightly larger raw Sharpe ratio of 0.675, the alpha produced by this method was not statistically significant ($p=0.24$), and the maximum drawdown of $-$35.6\% was significantly worse than that of DHRP ($-$27.5\%), indicating that the higher returns generated by Markowitz's method were primarily due to concentrated factor bets rather than true diversification. Both classical HRP and equal weighting fell in the middle of the performance distribution producing near-zero and statistically insignificant alphas entirely explained by their passive factor exposures.

In the commodities universe, DHRP produced its largest gain in terms of Sharpe ratio (0.875) and produced an annualized alpha of 11.9\% that was statistically significant at the one percent level ($t = 3.01$; $p = 0.003$; Newey-West standard errors). The $R^2$ value of the six-factor model used to estimate these parameters was very low (0.134) indicating that commodity returns contain relatively little exposure to the standard equity risk premia. Therefore, the fact that the alpha generated by DHRP is truly orthogonal to the standard systematic factors adds considerable importance to this finding. Furthermore, the commodity universe contains ten assets across various categories including precious metals, energy, agriculture and industrial metals creating rich hierarchical diversification opportunities. Therefore, DHRP is ideally suited to take advantage of these types of opportunities.

For the emerging markets universe, however, there is very little differentiation among the methods tested. All eight classical and DHRP-family methods fall in a narrow Sharpe ratio range of 0.089 to 0.237 and none generate statistically significant alpha. The average pairwise correlation among the ten EM assets exceeds 0.55, sharply limiting the potential for any allocation strategy to distinguish itself: when assets move nearly in lockstep, weighting choices have second-order impact relative to the common factor exposure. Hierarchical inductive bias therefore appears to provide its greatest benefits when the correlation structure of the underlying universe contains genuine diversification opportunities, and offers little advantage when it does not.

We additionally tested a multimodal extension (LLM-DHRP) that fuses FinBERT \citep{Huang2023, Araci2019} embeddings of financial news with Qwen3-8B \citep{Qwen2025} structured sentiment features through residual gated cross-attention, and we report the result transparently as a negative finding: the extension fails to improve over the base DHRP layer in all three universes and erases the only highly significant headline result of the paper, with the commodity-universe alpha collapsing from $p = 0.003$ to $p = 0.190$. Section~\ref{sec:llmablation} provides a four-mechanism failure-mode diagnosis (firm-level vs.\ aggregate-ETF granularity mismatch, cross-sectional sentiment averaging, routing-bottleneck capacity limits, and macro-structural drivers in commodities orthogonal to firm-level news consistent with the limits-of-arbitrage view of \citet{ShleiferVishny1997}). We retain LLM-DHRP in the paper not as a contribution but as a cautionary, well-diagnosed counterexample, in keeping with the negative-result reporting norms encouraged by recent venues such as the \emph{I Can't Believe It's Not Better} (ICBINB) workshop at NeurIPS.

There are several limitations worth noting. First, although our 10-year data window and $\approx 9$-year rolling-window evaluation cover multiple distinct macro regimes (pre-COVID, COVID crash, recovery, rate hikes, post-hike), it remains a single historical realization. Our multi-seed robustness analysis (Section~4.8) partially addresses this concern by showing stability across random initializations, and the released code base supports a strict held-out OOS protocol for further reproducibility, but neither eliminates sample dependence. Second, while our transaction cost sensitivity analysis (Section~4.9) demonstrates that DHRP maintains positive net-of-cost Sharpe ratios at realistic cost levels, we do not model market impact or liquidity constraints that would arise at institutional scale. Third, the emerging markets universe is dominated by a high common-factor structure ($\bar{\rho} > 0.55$), which may suppress the discriminatory power of any allocation method including DHRP and may not generalize to lower-correlation EM compositions. Fourth, the commodity-universe alpha of 11.9\% is computed against an equity-based factor model with low explanatory power ($R^2 = 0.134$); a commodity-native factor model based on carry, value, and momentum \citep{AsnessMoskowitzPedersen2013} would constitute a stronger robustness check. Fifth, our negative result on text fusion (Section~\ref{sec:llmablation}) is restricted to FinBERT and Qwen3-8B sentiment averaged across all assets at the ETF aggregation level; instrument-conditioned text retrieval, asset-level news streams, larger or domain-fine-tuned encoders, or attention mechanisms over per-asset text channels (rather than a single global pooled vector) may yield different conclusions, and we do not claim that hierarchical allocators are intrinsically incompatible with language-model features.

Future research should explore: (i)~testing DHRP on larger and more diverse asset universes including individual equities and fixed income; (ii)~integrating commodity-specific carry, value, and momentum factors for a more appropriate benchmark in non-equity universes; (iii)~exploring alternative text sources such as central bank communications, earnings transcripts, and macroeconomic nowcasts to improve LLM-DHRP's consistency across universes; and (iv)~developing adaptive regime detection mechanisms that modulate HRP regularization strength based on detected market conditions.
